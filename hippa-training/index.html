🔐 What is PHI & HIPAA?

Protected Health Information

Also referred to as “Personal health information”

It means any kind of information in the medical record or designated record set that can be used to identify an individual and that was created, used, or disclosed in the course of providing a health care service such as diagnosis or treatment.

A PHI includes an individual's:

Demographic information Financial information Medical histories, test and laboratory results, Mental health conditions Insurance information, Social security number and Other data that a healthcare professional collects to identify an individual and determine appropriate care.

🚨 Why is PHI so valuable?

PHI is valuable because it contains both medical and personal identity information. Criminals use it for fraud, identity theft, fake insurance claims, or illegal drug access.

Misuse of PHI Such as: Cyber fraud, blackmailing by using personal information, Financial information and SSN.

De-identified Data

De-identified data means health information that cannot be used to identify an individual. Once PHI is properly de-identified, it is no longer considered PHI under HIPAA, and the Privacy Rule no longer applies to it.

HIPAA

“Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act” of 1996.

HIPAA laws are a series of federal regulatory standards outlining the lawful use and disclosure of protected health information in the United States. HIPAA compliance is regulated by the Department of Health and Human Services (HHS) and enforced by the Office for Civil Rights (OCR).

  • HIPAA compliance is essential in the healthcare industry to ensure the protection of patients' sensitive health information (PHI).
  • The main purpose of HIPAA measures is to protect the health information and medical privacy of the patients.

👥 Who Needs to be HIPAA Compliant?

Anyone handling Electronic Protected Health Information (ePHI)

Anyone who creates, receives, maintains, or transmits electronic protected health information (ePHI) must comply with HIPAA rules.

The HIPAA Rules apply to:

Covered Entities & Business Associates.

Covered Entities (CE)

Directly associated with PHI

A covered entity is one of the following - A healthcare provider, A health Plan, A healthcare Clearinghouse; but only if they transmit any information in an electronic form in connection with a transaction for which HHS has adopted a standard.

Healthcare Provider

Doctors, Clinics, Psychologists, Dentists, Chiropractors, Nursing Homes, Pharmacies

Health Plan

Health insurance companies, HMOs, Company health plans, Government programs that pay for health care, such as Medicare, Medicaid, and the military and veterans health care programs

Healthcare Clearinghouse

Entities that process nonstandard health information they receive from another entity into a standard format (e.g., billing services).

Overall, HIPAA compliance is a shared responsibility between covered entities and their business associates to ensure the confidentiality, integrity, and availability of PHI.

Business Associates (BA)

Any outside entity that handles PHI on behalf of a covered entity is considered a business associate.

They perform certain functions on behalf of a covered entity that involve the use or disclosure of PHI.

  • third-party billing companies
  • IT providers: Dev, QA etc
  • lawyers, accountants, and consultants

Examples of Business Associates:

A third party administrator that assists a health plan with claims processing. A CPA firm whose accounting services to a health care provider involve access to protected health information. An attorney whose legal services to a health plan involve access to protected health information. A consultant that performs utilization reviews for a hospital. *Note: Utilization review helps determine if healthcare services are being used efficiently and appropriately to provide patients with the care they need. A health care clearinghouse that translates a claim from a non-standard format into a standard transaction on behalf of a health care provider and forwards the processed transaction to a payer.

📜 HIPAA Compliance Rules

Key Points of HIPAA Rules

  • Privacy Rule: Regulates the use and disclosure of PHI
  • Security Rule: Physical, Technical, and Administrative security measures
  • Enforcement Rule: Provides instruction for regulating liability and imposing penalties for violations
  • Breach Notification Rule: Guidelines for how and when to report violations
  • Omnibus Rule: Outlines how CE or BA should handle PHI, enhance the privacy and security rules from unauthorized access or disclosure

Deep Dive into the Rules

Privacy Rule

  • Protects patients’ medical records and PHI.
  • Grants individuals rights over their health data (access, correction, restrictions).
  • Limits use/disclosure of PHI without patient authorization.

Security Rule

Requires safeguards for ePHI (electronic PHI).

  • Administrative: Risk analysis, workforce training, security policies.
  • Physical: Facility access controls, workstation/device security.
  • Technical: Encryption, access controls, audit logs, authentication.

Breach Notification Rule

  • Guidelines for how and when to report violations
  • Requires notification to affected individuals, HHS, and sometimes media within 60 days of discovering a breach.

Enforcement Rule

  • Defines penalties for non-compliance (civil and criminal).
  • include fines, corrective action plans, and in extreme cases, criminal charges
  • Fines range from $100 to $50,000 per violation, with an annual cap of $1.5M+, depending on negligence level.

🌐 HITECH

The Health Information Technology for Economic and Clinical Health Act (HITECH) - It was designed to promote the adoption and meaningful use of health information technology (HIT) and electronic health records (EHRs) in the United States healthcare system. Introduced under the ARRA (American Recovery and Reinvestment Act) to strengthen HIPAA and promote adoption of health IT.

HIPAA lays the foundation for protecting patient data, while HITECH strengthened enforcement and accelerated secure adoption of digital health technologies like EHRs. Together, they ensure that compliance and security remain central to U.S. healthcare operations.

✅ HIPAA Compliance Checklist

For Software Development Team

📖

Understand HIPAA Regulations

Familiarize yourself and your team with HIPAA

🔑

Implement Access Controls

access to PHI is restricted to authorized personnel only.

🔐

Data Encryption

Use encryption techniques for securing databases, file storage, and backups.

📝

Audit Trails

Implement audit logging- audit logs are secure, tamper-proof, and regularly reviewed.

🛡️

Secure Development Practices

Secure coding practices and update software libraries and dependencies regularly

📉

Data Minimization

Implement data anonymization and pseudonymization techniques to reduce the risk associated with storing PHI.

📡

Secure Transmission of Data

Secure communication protocols (e.g., HTTPS) for transmitting PHI over networks.

🤝

Business Associate Agreements (BAAs)

sign a Business Associate Agreement (BAA) outlining responsibilities for protecting PHI.

♻️

Disaster Recovery and Contingency Planning

in the event of a data breach or system failure. Regularly backup PHI and test the restoration process.

🎓

Employee Training and Awareness

Provide comprehensive training to employees, regular updation of policies and compliance requirements

🔍

Regular Security Assessments

Conduct regular security assessments, penetration testing, and vulnerability scans, org. wide risk assessment

🚨

Incident Response Plan

response plan outlining procedures for responding to security incidents, data breaches, and unauthorized disclosures of PHI.

🚨 Violation & Penalty Structure

Real-World Impact

Last year 15,000 violations were reported.

Real Incident:

In one incident, a thief used stolen medical records to impersonate a patient and rack up over $20,000 in medical bills in that patient’s name. The victim was still dealing with the fallout 15 years later, worrying that hospital records might list the thief’s blood type instead of his own.

HIPAA Violation

A HIPAA violation is when a HIPAA-covered entity or a business associate fails to comply with one or more of the provisions of the HIPAA Privacy, Security, or Breach Notification Rules.

Failure to perform an organization-wide risk analysis Failure to enter into a HIPAA-compliant business associate agreement Wrongful disclosures of PHI or failure to safeguard PHI Delayed breach notifications A health care employee accidentally viewing the records of a patient. Accessing PHI with intent to sell it for profit or personal gain. A staff member discusses treatment plans with a patient in a waiting room or in front of other people without the patient’s permission.

Besides human error, other common violations are:

Lack of encryption Data breaches caused by a security hack or phishing attack Unauthorized access Improper disposal of records Loss or theft of devices

OCR determines a number of factors when determining penalties such as

  • the length of time a violation was allowed to persist
  • the number of people affected
  • the nature of the data exposed

Tier Breakdown & Real Examples

Tier 1

A violation that the covered entity was unaware of and could not have realistically avoided.

Example: Car stolen having medical record laptop.

Tier 2

Violation should have been known but not avoided.

Example: Sending record to unencrypted message platform.

Tier 3

Willful neglect where correction attempt was made.

Example: Discussing treatment plan with co-nursing.

Tier 4

Willful neglect with no correction within 30 days.

🛡️ How to avoid HIPAA Violation

To comply with the HIPAA Security Rule, the CDC requires all covered entities to:

  • Ensure the confidentiality, integrity, and availability of all electronic PHI (ePHI).
  • Detect and safeguard against anticipated threats to the security of the information.
  • Protect against anticipated impermissible uses or disclosures.
  • Certify compliance by their workforce.
  • Proper use and disposal of PHI.
  • Technical security.

Covered Entities (CEs) and Business Associates (BAs) must limit the use, disclosure, and access to PHI to the minimum necessary to accomplish the intended purpose.

Objective:
Reduce unnecessary or excessive exposure of PHI, thereby lowering privacy/security risks.

Minimum Necessary Rule- HIPAA

🚀 ONC Cures Act Final Rule

Issued by: Office of the National Coordinator for Health IT (ONC)

Purpose: To give patients easier, faster, and more secure access to their electronic health information (EHI) and to promote interoperability between healthcare systems.

Empowering patients with control over their health data, promote innovation, and make healthcare systems more connected and transparent.

4 important Provisions of ONC Cures ACT, Final Rule

These provisions are specifically applicable for Health IT developers and healthcare providers

Information Blocking Prohibition

To adopt the practices that enable patients to access their health information.

Interoperability

Must implement standardized data exchange protocols to ensure seamless and secure sharing of EHI across different systems and platforms.

API functionality

To implement APIs that allow patients to access and exchange their health information through third-party applications.

Certification

To certify their products to ensure they meet the technical and functional requirements outlined in the rule.

📣 Other HIPAA Rules

HITECH

Health Information Technology for Economic and Clinical Health Act

TRAINING AND POLICIES

Training to employees who handle PHI and implement policies and procedures.

AUDITS AND ASSESSMENTS

Periodic audits and risk assessments are essential to identify vulnerabilities.

Breach notification requirements:

The following information should be provided when reporting a PHI breach to OCR:

Name Full address Telephone number (with area code) Email address Information on the organization or individual who you believed violated health information privacy rights A description of how those privacy rights were violated Your signature and the date of the complaint
  • If ten (or fewer) people were exposed, written or telephone notice must be given to the affected parties.
  • If 500 (or more) people were exposed, businesses must notify the local media within 60 days.
  • In both cases, the company responsible for the breach must fill out an electronic breach form to report to The Secretary.

📋 SUMMARY

  • Risk Analysis is mandatory under HIPAA/HITECH—ongoing evaluation of threats/vulnerabilities to PHI/ePHI.
  • Access control, Audit Logging, and Encryption are critical technical safeguards.
  • Workforce training is essential to prevent human error breaches.
  • Vendors (BAs) must sign Business Associate Agreements (BAAs) to ensure compliance.
  • Non-compliance risks: Hefty fines, reputational damage, loss of patient trust, and potential criminal charges.
  • Reduce unnecessary or excessive exposure of PHI, thereby lowering privacy/security risks- MINIMUM NECESSARY RULE
  • Access Control = Who can get the access ? (Limit to minimum necessary).
  • Audit Logging = Who did what, when, and why? (Track and monitor).
  • Data Provenance = Where did the data come from and how did it change over time?
  • Auditability = Who touched the data, when, and what did they do?
Data Provenance + Auditability = Integrity & Trust
